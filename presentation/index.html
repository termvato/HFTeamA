02 / 10
Desk robots today are trained for one chore with the objects they saw. One independent test on this very arm got 30 to 40 percent success after 50 demonstrations. The arm itself is the cheap part, about a hundred dollars. Putting a second one to work is the hard part: new data, or re-programming. And more arms is never N times faster: published systems get 1.5 to 3 times, with nothing telling you why. Ours needs no training per object: a camera model names what is on the desk. (~35 s) Sources if asked: Tiensmith 2026 blog test (ACT about 40%, SmolVLA and pi0.5 about 30%); Hugging Face launch price; our research report for the 1.5 to 3 times range.

How it works

One loop, any arm

The one contract every arm speaks

execute(task) → ok | failed | timeout | arm_down
Real, simulated and fake arms all implement it.

Ops · 01
Camera + VLM

Qwen3-VL points at each object; ArUco markers map pixels onto the table.

Ops · 02
Planner

Reach: only arms that can get to an object are offered it. Time: every step has an estimate, replaced by each arm's measured times.

Ops · 03
Executor

One arm at a time in a shared area. Retries, heartbeat, and a re-plan on every event.

Action · 04
Arm workers

SkillWorker: IK skills on MuJoCo and LeRobot; a trained X-VLA policy fits the same call.

Discovery

07 / 10
Speed-up is never N times. The finish time can't beat the work split across arms, the longest chain of ordered steps, or the busiest shared area. We compute that bound on every re-plan and say which one binds. This chart is planned, not measured on real arms: our planner on today's code for a 12-object sort: the second arm nearly halves it, the third helps less, and the fourth adds nothing because everyone queues at the shared input bin.
