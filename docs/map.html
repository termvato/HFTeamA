HFTeamA Orchestrator Map

Plug in more SO-101 arms and the batch finishes sooner. When an arm is added, removed or fails, the orchestrator re-splits the remaining work, shows the new finish time, and names what stops further speed-up. The core knows no task: each task is a folder of steps, order and shared zones.

How the parts connect

built and tested to build

Three shared formats are fixed so people can build in parallel: the task graph, the arm contract execute(task) → Result (ok, grasp_fail, timeout, arm_down), and the event log.

When an arm is plugged in

  1. Discovery sees a known USB serial and looks it up in the arm registry (id, calibration, base position, reach).
  2. The arm's worker starts, reads pose, temperatures and torque, and checks targets are inside joint limits and above the table.
  3. The arm moves slowly to home, reports READY, and an arm_added event goes to the executor.
  4. The executor re-plans the remaining work. Tasks already running stay where they are. CP-SAT gets 1 to 2 seconds; HEFT takes over if it runs out.
  5. The screen updates: new bars on the Gantt, a new predicted finish time, and the limit that now binds.

Removing an arm is a slow stop: press "remove arm N", the arm finishes or aborts its skill and parks, then the cable comes out. Its unfinished tasks go back to the others. A dropped part is caught by the grasp check and retried.

Why the speed-up curve bends

Finish time can never beat the largest of three numbers: total work divided by arms, the longest chain of ordered steps, and the busiest shared zone. The bar colour shows which one binds. Values are lower bounds in seconds from the code, with placeholder durations until we time the real arms.

Later extension

Two-hand steps unlocked by a second arm. A step that needs two arms (hold and insert, handoff) stays blocked while one arm is plugged in; plugging in arm 2 makes it runnable, so a new arm adds capability as well as speed. Fits the current task graph via needs_arms: 2. Not in the core build.
