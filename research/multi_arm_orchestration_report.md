# Schedule N arms like a job shop

A 48-hour team should build a **classical scheduler wrapped in a thin LLM front end**: an LLM (or a hand-written file) turns the order into a validated JSON task graph, Google OR-Tools CP-SAT assigns every subtask to an arm and a time slot to minimise makespan, and an event-driven executor fires each task when its predecessors, its arm and its shared zones are free, re-solving in about a second when something fails. Handoffs, hold-and-insert and two-arm grasps are not a separate research problem here; each becomes two or three interval constraints in the same CP-SAT model, and the solver cannot produce a deadlocked plan. The honest expectation is **roughly 1.5-3x speed-up with 2-4 arms, not N-times**, because every published multi-arm system lands there ([Stop-N-Go](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.07606v1); [ScheduleStream](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04758)), and the demo should show the curve bending and explain why with a lower bound computed live. What is genuinely new is not "LLM splits a task across arms" (done since 2023) but **measured wall-clock makespan on real low-cost arms, with precedence, unequal reach, physical collaboration and live re-splitting after a failure**: RoboBallet, the strongest published system, is simulation-only and handles neither ordered tasks nor robots with different capabilities ([The Batch](https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/google-deepminds-roboballet-research-project-blends-graph-neural-networks-with-reinforcement-learning-to-drive-8-armed-robots)), and no shipping industrial product re-splits work across arms automatically. The recommended rig is **two real SO-101s forming one physical collaborative cell plus N simulated SO-101s in MuJoCo** sharing the same orchestrator and Gantt chart, with scripted IK skills on the arms rather than learned policies.

Throughout, "(inference, not checked)" marks reasoning that no source or local run backs. LeRobot facts were checked against a local clone of LeRobot 0.6.2 (commit `d3793b7`, 23 Oct 2026).

## Arm-splitting is a flexible job shop with zones and sync points

The problem has a precise name. In multi-robot task allocation terms it is single-task robots, single-robot tasks, time-extended assignment with cross-schedule dependencies (ST-SR-TA, class XD in Korsah's iTax), which becomes ST-MR-TA as soon as a step needs two arms ([Sims et al. survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.09650); [Gerkey and Matarić](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs689/papers/gerkey04formal.pdf)). In operations-research terms it is a **flexible job shop, or multi-mode resource-constrained project scheduling, over a precedence DAG**: an arm is a machine, a subtask is a task with one "mode" per arm that can reach it (each with its own duration), a task-graph edge is a precedence constraint with an optional minimum lag, and a shared region of the table (a bin, a fixture, a handoff tray) is a capacity-1 resource. The objective is makespan, Cmax. The closest published analogue is MIT's Tercio, built for robot teams in aircraft final assembly with temporal and **spatial-proximity constraints**; its authors note that optimal scheduling of three or more such tasks is strongly NP-hard ([Gombolay, T-RO 2018](https://people.csail.mit.edu/gombolay/Publications/Gombolay_TRO_2018.pdf)). Arm travel between task locations is formally a sequence-dependent setup time; folding a fixed approach-and-retract time into each task's duration is the cheap, adequate approximation at tabletop scale (inference, not checked).

### CP-SAT is the default solver, with HEFT as baseline and fallback

**CP-SAT, OR-Tools' open-source constraint solver, is the right engine.** Google's own `flexible_job_shop_sat.py` example already contains the core: one main interval per task, one optional interval per candidate machine with a presence literal, `add_exactly_one` over those literals, `add_no_overlap` per machine, precedence as `start >= previous_end`, and makespan via `add_max_equality` then `minimize` ([OR-Tools example](https://github.com/google/or-tools/blob/stable/examples/python/flexible_job_shop_sat.py)). On a 9,280-instance benchmark, CP-SAT came within **0.68% of best-known on flexible job shops with a 1.04% optimality gap, against 0.99% and 27.67% for IBM's commercial CP Optimizer**; the same study found that modelling choices matter, with an earlier OR-Tools FJSP model scoring 7.55% ([PyJobShop, arXiv 2502.13483](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13483v1)). Constraint programming also beats MILP by one to two orders of magnitude on small scheduling instances of this shape ([arXiv 2006.13064](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.13064)), and Tercio's own analysis shows why: sequencing binaries grow as O(|τ|²) with O(|τ|!) permutations, so MILP chokes when tasks outnumber agents ([Gombolay](https://people.csail.mit.edu/gombolay/Publications/Gombolay_TRO_2018.pdf)). PyJobShop wraps CP-SAT neatly but **does not support tasks that need several resources at once** ([PyJobShop](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13483v1)), so a team doing two-arm steps should write raw OR-Tools (about 150 lines, inference, not checked). No source gives CP-SAT solve times at exactly 2-8 arms and 10-50 tasks; since benchmark instances several times larger reach about 1% gaps, a 5-10 s limit should give optimal or near-optimal plans at hackathon scale (inference, not checked), and the team should benchmark this in the first evening.

Alongside it, build **HEFT** (Heterogeneous Earliest Finish Time) in roughly 40 lines. It ranks tasks by critical-path distance to the end of the graph, then places each on the worker that finishes it earliest, inserting into idle gaps ([HEFT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterogeneous_earliest_finish_time)). HEFT never fails and runs in milliseconds, so it serves three roles: the baseline the pitch compares against, the warm-start hint for CP-SAT, and the online fallback if the solver times out. Greedy list scheduling carries a famous trap worth putting on a slide: in Graham's 9-job example, **adding a machine raised the makespan from 12 to 15**, and shortening every job raised it to 13 ([List scheduling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_scheduling)). An exact solver run to optimality cannot get worse with an extra arm, since it may ignore that arm (inference, not checked, and not guaranteed under a time limit). Auctions such as CBBA earn their keep only when decentralisation is the selling point, and learned schedulers cost days of training: RoboBallet needed 6-24 hours to reach 95% task solution, with gains continuing for 2-6 days ([RoboBallet](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.05397v1)).

### Shared zones become mutex resources; collaboration becomes interval relations

Coordination has a scheduling layer and a motion layer. At the scheduling layer, **a shared zone is booked over time intervals**, exactly as the CP model of ABB's dual-arm YuMi does with non-overlapping zone reservations ([Constraints 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10601-023-09345-4)) and as Tercio does with big-M disjunctions between tasks that are too close ([Gombolay](https://people.csail.mit.edu/gombolay/Publications/Gombolay_TRO_2018.pdf)). At the motion layer, the simplest robust method is to plan each arm independently and resolve conflicts only by inserting pauses: Stop-N-Go did this on four UR5 arms and got **26-43 s makespans against 59-92 s sequential, with 90-93% success versus 37-43% for coupled RRT-Connect** ([Stop-N-Go](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.07606v1)). The hackathon version of both is the same idea at two levels: split the table into each arm's private area plus a few overlap zones, add one `add_no_overlap` per shared zone over the tasks that sweep it, and enforce the same thing at runtime with a zone lock so an arm waits rather than collides when real durations drift. Coarse zones discard some parallelism through false conflicts, but they are easy to verify, and no source compares them against trajectory-level coordination for small tabletop arms.

Collaboration fits the same model. **A two-arm step is one presence literal shared by two optional intervals on an allowed arm pair, with equal starts and ends**; each arm's NoOverlap then blocks both. A handoff is a short two-arm interval in the handoff zone, chained by precedence: A picks, then A and B hand off together, then B places. Hold-and-insert is Allen's "during" relation: the holding interval starts no later and ends no earlier than the insertion (all three encodings inference, not checked). The real danger with cooperative steps is deadlock. Bischoff et al. prove that **a plan with cooperative tasks and precedence is executable if and only if the combined graph of all arms' sequences plus precedence edges is acyclic**, and that standard local-search moves can silently break this ([arXiv 2005.03902](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.03902)). A CP solver simply finds no assignment for a cyclic wait; HEFT or any hand-written greedy repair must check acyclicity explicitly.

Collaboration costs throughput. A two-arm step consumes double arm-time and adds waiting for the partner, so it pays only when it makes an impossible step possible (reach, weight, fixturing without a jig) or shortens the critical path, for instance when a handoff replaces one arm's long pick-carry-place chain (inference, not checked). No source quantifies how much makespan handoffs save; dual-arm rearrangement work claims "significant time savings" over greedy and naive parallel plans without numbers in the abstract ([Gao and Yu, arXiv 2207.08078](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08078)).

### Speed-up has a computable ceiling, and the demo should display it

**Makespan can never beat max(total arm-work / N, critical-path length, busiest-zone load)**, and Graham-style list scheduling guarantees at most W/N + (1 − 1/N)·L, within a factor 2 − 1/N of optimal ([arXiv 2004.09682](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.09682); [List scheduling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_scheduling)). Speed-up therefore saturates at W/L, the parallelism of the task graph, which is Amdahl's law for arms; zones lower the ceiling further. With two-arm steps the work term becomes Σ kᵢ·pᵢ / N, where kᵢ is the number of arms step i needs (inference, not checked). Empirically, RoboBallet's 20-task runs dropped from about 7.5 s with 4 arms to 4.5 s with 8, which the paper labels a 60% reduction but is arithmetically 40%, and the authors note a density limit "beyond which there would be no benefit" ([RoboBallet](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.05397v1)). Showing the achieved makespan next to this lower bound, and naming which term binds (arms, graph or zone), turns "it got faster" into an engineering argument. CP-SAT's own best bound supplies the same number with an optimality proof, which lets the team say "provably within X% of optimal".

### Execute a partial order, re-solve on real disruptions

Fixed timestamps break on the first slow grasp. Tercio's answer is to compile the schedule into a **dispatchable temporal network**, which absorbs small delays with one-step propagation and cut re-computation after disturbances by up to 75% ([Gombolay](https://people.csail.mit.edu/gombolay/Publications/Gombolay_TRO_2018.pdf)). The hackathon version keeps only the solver's decisions (which arm, per-arm order, per-zone order) and fires each task the moment its predecessors, arm and zones are free. On a real disruption (a failed grasp, a dead arm, a large overrun) the executor removes finished tasks, freezes running ones as fixed intervals, re-inserts a retry task or re-routes it to another arm that can reach it, and re-solves CP-SAT warm-started from the old plan with `AddHint` and a 1-2 s limit, dropping to HEFT on timeout ([or-tools-discuss](https://groups.google.com/g/or-tools-discuss/c/8SemJDNHcTY)). The `repair_hint` parameter exists but its semantics were not verified. Durations should be padded to a high percentile (p80) of measured per-arm times, since a finishing-early task can still lengthen a greedy re-dispatch ([List scheduling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_scheduling)).

## The literature converged on "LLM writes a DAG, a scheduler runs it"

Research since 2022 moved in one clear direction. **RoCo (2023)** gave each arm its own LLM agent that "discusses" the plan, then a centralised multi-arm RRT executes it; dialog lost badly on the high-overlap packing task (0.44 success against 0.82 for a central planner), and the paper reports no wall-clock time at all, noting that per-turn LLM latency "hurts dynamic or speed-sensitive tasks" ([RoCo](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.04738)). **SMART-LLM (2023-24)** decomposed, formed coalitions and allocated across 1-4 robots in AI2-THOR via Python threads, with no timing metric and a complex-task success of 0.48 ± 0.40 across five GPT-4 runs ([SMART-LLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.10062)). COHERENT and LaMMA-P (ICRA 2025) improved success and step counts in simulation ([COHERENT](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.15146v3); [LaMMA-P](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.20560)). Then the pattern flipped: **DART-LLM** builds an explicit dependency DAG ([DART-LLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09022v2)), **RoboPARA** schedules an LLM-built DAG across two arms with deadlock rollback and claims 30-50% shorter execution ([RoboPARA](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.06683v2)), and **OSDAG (June 2026)** uses a single LLM call to emit a DAG with precedence, capability and resource constraints, then an online scheduler hands ready tasks to idle robots, reporting **up to 38% lower makespan and 5-15x faster reasoning than dialogue methods** ([OSDAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15255)). A 2026 survey of 49 LLM multi-robot systems found only one in manufacturing and advises keeping LLM calls "sparse, event-triggered" rather than in the control loop ([LLM-MRS survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.03814)).

Classical task-and-motion planning got to makespan earlier and more rigorously. Chen et al. (2022) allocated arms by MILP for assemblies of up to 23 parts, with high-quality makespans at substantial compute cost ([arXiv 2203.02475](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02475)). NVIDIA's **ScheduleStream (2025-26) already plans multi-arm makespan on SO100 arms in simulation**: on four SO100s it cut makespan from 7.8 to 3.6 (first solution), with GPU sampling giving 99% success and 1.9 s to first solution, but its real-world results are 21 qualitative demos on a two-arm Kinova ([ScheduleStream](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04758)). RoboBallet (DeepMind with Intrinsic, Science Robotics 2025) jointly learns allocation, scheduling and motion for 8 Franka arms and 40 reaching tasks, but only reaching, only in simulation ([RoboBallet](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.05397v1)). On the learned-policy side, bimanual systems such as ACT/ALOHA reach 80-90% on fine two-arm tasks by treating both arms as one body ([ACT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.13705.pdf)), which does not extend to three or more independent arms, and LeRobot's only multi-arm construct is the fixed left/right `bi_so_follower` pair ([Seeed dual-arm guide](https://wiki.seeedstudio.com/lerobot_double_arm_so_arm_training/)).

That map makes the novelty line sharp. **Not new, and a judge who reads arXiv will know it:** LLM decomposition across arms, LLM-to-DAG-to-parallel schedule, makespan-optimal multi-arm planning on SO-10x arms in simulation, and learned N-arm motion. **Defensible as new**, because the literature consistently lacks it: real-hardware wall-clock makespan on low-cost arms with success rates and stated baselines (no 2025-26 LLM or TAMP scheduler found reports this); live re-dispatch after a failure during parallel execution, measured as seconds lost per failure (CoMuRoS, a July 2026 closed-loop framework and REPAIR only began to appear, and were seen only in snippets) ([CoMuRoS](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.22354v1); [arXiv 2607.06990](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.06990)); **per-arm durations predicted by a system-identified digital twin** instead of assumed, which matters because cheap arms differ; and an open orchestration layer that dispatches LeRobot skills. The absence of real SO-10x multi-arm makespan results rests on search absence, not an exhaustive check (inference, not checked).

## Industry ships fixed programs, not live re-splitting

Commercial "multi-robot orchestration" in manufacturing means one of four things, and none of them is automatic task splitting across arms. **Vendor controllers synchronise hand-written programs**: ABB MultiMove runs up to 4 robots on one controller in "independent" or "coordinated" mode (one holds, one works), programmed by hand in RAPID with SyncMove ([ABB MultiMove](https://library.e.abb.com/public/734fb908d1c8ee50c12576dd005b69d0/ABB%20MultiMove%20functionality.pdf)), and DELMIA's 2025 release added simulation of such already-defined synchronised programs, not allocation ([3DS community](https://3dswym.3dexperience.3ds.com/post/delmia-virtual-factory-robotics/new-in-r2025xfd01-support-for-abb-multimove-simulation-and-olp_qNEo9i_QTheZgFcwyuNkfQ)). **Cell platforms connect and sequence equipment**: Flexxbotics' 2025 headline feature is one robot tending several machines ([Robotics Tomorrow](https://www.roboticstomorrow.com/news/2025/03/26/flexxbotics-unveils-latest-release-of-flexxcore-with-powerful-new-capabilities-for-multi-machine-robotic-automation/24516)), Vention's controller runs 20 motors over EtherCAT rather than several arms ([Robotics247](https://www.robotics247.com/article/pack-expo-2026-vention-introduces-24-7-video-based-remote-support/supply_chain)), and Mujin and Bright Machines sell orchestrated cells mainly in logistics and server assembly ([Automation World](https://www.automationworld.com/55297500); [The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/teradyne-invests-ibright-machines-brings-robotics-ai-infrastructure-manufacturing/)). **Digital-twin fleet testing** is NVIDIA's Mega blueprint, still in preview and built around the VDA5050 mobile-robot protocol ([NVIDIA](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/mega-omniverse-blueprint)). **Open-source fleet managers** such as Open-RMF were designed for mobile robots; a static arm enters only through a custom "workcell" node, and its maintainers list resource contention as an open problem ([Open-RMF discourse](https://discourse.openrobotics.org/t/open-rmf-integration-with-manipulators-668/44555); [ROSCon 2024](https://roscon.ros.org/2024/talks/OpenRMF_and_the_Challenge_of_Resource_Contention_in_Large-Scale_Robot_Fleets.pdf)).

The money is moving toward the gap. Intrinsic moved into Google in February 2026, showed an "Intelligence Cell" at Automate 2026 that lets integrators manage cells "without programming each robot", and open-sourced Intrinsic Core in September ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/06/22/intrinsic-unveils-next-gen-accessible-automated-industrial-ai-robotic-assembly/); [Intrinsic blog](https://www.intrinsic.ai/blog)). The pain is real but the vendor numbers are marketing: integration and programming are quoted at **1.5-3x the arm price by integrators and 4-6x by rule of thumb** ([Inbolt](https://www.inbolt.com/resources/what-are-integration-costs); [Engineering.com](https://www.engineering.com/story/time-and-money-how-much-do-industrial-robots-cost)), and plants standardise on one robot brand because each speaks its own language ([Robohub](https://robohub.org/the-future-of-robot-off-line-programming)). The cautionary tale is READY Robotics, whose cross-vendor ForgeOS shut down in August 2024 after raising $41.5M, partly because robot makers would not cooperate ([The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/ready-robotics-maker-of-forgeos-shuts-down/)).

The white space a hack can credibly occupy is therefore **one task list, automatically re-split live across arms with different reach, with precedence and physical collaboration, checked in a twin and run on real hardware**: exactly RoboBallet's admitted limits plus what MultiMove makes engineers hand-code. A hackathon cannot credibly claim anything about industrial controllers, safety certification or production throughput, and should say so before a judge does.

## One process per arm, one interface for real and simulated arms

### LeRobot runs N arms only if you build the N

LeRobot 0.6.2 has **no N-arm robot class**; the registry contains single arms and fixed bimanual pairs, and `bi_so_follower` reads and writes its left and right arms one after the other in the same thread ([bi_so_follower.py](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/d3793b7e36522321c1c455f0dc4826db3793dceb/src/lerobot/robots/bi_so_follower/bi_so_follower.py)). Each `SOFollower` takes its own `port`, gains, `max_relative_target` and `num_read_retries`, and each arm needs a unique `--robot.id`, which also names its calibration file ([config_so_follower.py](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/d3793b7e36522321c1c455f0dc4826db3793dceb/src/lerobot/robots/so_follower/config_so_follower.py); [so101.mdx](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/d3793b7e36522321c1c455f0dc4826db3793dceb/docs/source/so101.mdx)). Since every SO-101 is its own USB-serial Feetech bus at 1 Mbaud, the clean design is **one Python process per arm** owning one `SOFollower`, a skill executor and a heartbeat, talking to the orchestrator over a queue (multiprocessing, ZMQ or Zenoh); separate processes sidestep the GIL and confine a bus crash to one arm (inference, not checked on hardware). Pin each arm to a udev symlink by USB serial number, because `/dev/ttyACM*` order changes on replug (inference). Serial traffic is tiny next to USB 2.0; cameras are what saturate a controller, at roughly 18 MB/s for uncompressed 640x480 at 30 fps, so use MJPEG or one overhead camera (arithmetic inference, not checked). Expect occasional "Incorrect status packet" read errors, which LeRobot's own config says happen "especially when several joints move at once" and retries immediately. LeRobot's remote inference server admits **one robot session per process**, so learned policies on several arms mean several servers ([remote_inference README](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/d3793b7e36522321c1c455f0dc4826db3793dceb/src/lerobot/remote_inference/README.md)).

### Scripted IK skills beat learned policies for a throughput demo

A throughput demo is judged on whether the schedule completes, and one stuck grasp stalls the whole Gantt. Independent testing on SO-101 pick-and-place found **ACT at about 40%, SmolVLA and pi0.5 at about 30% with 50 demos**, and concluded "no policy was reliable" ([Tiensmith 2026](https://justintiensmith.github.io/vla-research/2026/04/10/are-vlas-overhyped-first-results-on-a-real-robot.html)); the SmolVLA authors report 78.3% on their own setup, a gap worth noting ([SmolVLA](https://huggingface.co/blog/smolvla)). LeRobot 0.6.2 ships placo-based IK steps (`InverseKinematicsEEToJoints`, `EEBoundsAndSafety`) under the `kinematics` extra ([robot_kinematic_processor.py](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/d3793b7e36522321c1c455f0dc4826db3793dceb/src/lerobot/robots/so_follower/robot_kinematic_processor.py)), which is enough for scripted pick, place, present, regrasp and insert at fixed or AprilTag-detected poses. The AI then lives where it is reliable: in task-graph generation and perception. If learned credibility is wanted, train ACT on one skill (50 episodes, a few hours on one GPU per the [ACT docs](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/d3793b7e36522321c1c455f0dc4826db3793dceb/docs/source/act.mdx)) with a scripted fallback, and keep it off the critical path (inference).

Collaboration on SO-101s should be scripted, time-synchronised primitives in one calibrated table frame. A **handoff** runs as a state machine with a barrier: A presents and holds, B approaches and closes, B's grasp check passes, and only then does A open; if B fails, A keeps holding and B retries once. **Hold-and-insert** needs a chamfered hole with about 2-3 mm clearance, because SO-101 repeatability will not do tight fits. **Shared carry** of a rigid part should be avoided, since two position-controlled arms holding one object fight each other (all inference, not checked). The cheapest robust shared frame is to bolt both bases to one board and **teach the handoff pose directly as stored joint vectors for both arms**, so the handoff does not depend on frame accuracy. Keep parts under about 100-150 g: LeRobot deliberately caps the gripper at `Max_Torque_Limit=500` and `Protection_Current=250` "to avoid burnout" ([so_follower.py](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/d3793b7e36522321c1c455f0dc4826db3793dceb/src/lerobot/robots/so_follower/so_follower.py)), and no grip-force figure under those caps was found. Check each follower's servo variant, since 7.4 V and 12 V STS3215s differ in stall torque, roughly 16.5-19.5 against 30 kg·cm ([Waveshare](https://www.waveshare.com/wiki/SO-ARM100/101)).

### MuJoCo puts 16 simulated arms next to the real ones cheaply

MuJoCo with Menagerie's Apache-licensed `robotstudio_so101` model is the fastest route ([Menagerie README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_menagerie/main/robotstudio_so101/README.md)). A local run with MuJoCo 3.17.0 attached N prefixed copies via `MjSpec.attach_body` and stepped free arms at **155x real time with 4 arms, 83x with 8 and 41x with 16** on one CPU thread (local run; no contacts or rendering, so pick-and-place and 30 fps rendering will cost more; script at `research/scripts/multi_arm_bench.py`). Isaac Lab ships an `SO101_CFG` and LeIsaac adds SO-101 tasks including bimanual ones, but both are heavier to set up ([NVIDIA DLI](https://docs.nvidia.com/learning/physical-ai/getting-started-with-isaac-lab/latest/train-a-robot-arm/02-so101-robot.html); [LeIsaac docs](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/d3793b7e36522321c1c455f0dc4826db3793dceb/docs/source/envhub_leisaac.mdx)). The key design move is that **every arm worker exposes the same `execute(skill) -> result` interface whether its backend is a real `SOFollower` or a MuJoCo copy**, and each real arm drives a "ghost" copy in the scene from its read joint positions. Simulated arms must run at 1x wall clock so their bars on the Gantt are comparable, while a separate faster-than-real-time sweep generates the N = 1..16 scaling curve offline (inference). A calibrated twin of Vato's arm, used as a black box that predicts per-skill durations, can make simulated skill durations more defensible than stock Menagerie defaults.

### Rerun for engineers, a Plotly timeline for judges

LeRobot pins `rerun-sdk>=0.24,<0.34`, and the installed 0.33.1 has `StateTimelineView` with `rr.StateChange`, which draws horizontal coloured lanes per entity: a native "actual" Gantt per arm in the viewer LeRobot already uses ([pyproject.toml](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/d3793b7e36522321c1c455f0dc4826db3793dceb/pyproject.toml); local docstring check). Both archetypes are flagged unstable, and whether future-dated "plan" lanes render well is untested, so pin the version. For the stage screen, a Plotly `px.timeline` in Dash with **ghosted plan bars, solid actual bars, hatched zone-wait bars and red failure markers** reads instantly (inference, not checked for live-update performance). Rerun's GraphView can show the task DAG coloured by status, and its 3D view the real arms next to the simulated ones.

### Failure detection comes from the gripper, recovery from the orchestrator

Grasp success can be read from the gripper itself: commanded closed, it **stalls short of closed with elevated current if it holds something and reaches near-closed if empty**, the same logic Robotiq grippers use, and a pickup check must be repeated during transport because it only proves the object was held at check time ([Robotiq](https://blog.robotiq.com/how-to-use-the-object-detection-feature-of-the-robotiq-grippers)). LeRobot already reads `Present_Current` via `bus.sync_read` ([joint_observations_processor.py](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/d3793b7e36522321c1c455f0dc4826db3793dceb/src/lerobot/rl/joint_observations_processor.py)); thresholds must be measured per arm from about ten empty and ten loaded closes, since no published STS3215 values exist (inference). Add a per-skill timeout at about 1.5x predicted duration and a heartbeat per arm process; a missed heartbeat marks the arm DOWN and returns its tasks to the pool (inference). Recovery belongs to the orchestrator, not inside skills: re-queue to the same arm or another arm that can reach, re-solve, and show the replan on the Gantt. A stopped arm should be parked by a slow, pre-checked motion rather than a torque cut with the arm raised (inference).

## An honest demo shows the curve bend, and why

Winning embodied-AI hackathon projects in 2025 emphasised "long-horizon completion, generalization, dual-arm coordination, and multi-robot collaboration", and every documented winner used learned policies on single long tasks ([Seeed recap](https://seeed.cc/post/2025-embodied-ai-hackathon-recap)); no SO-101 project demonstrating throughput scaling across N arms was found, so the angle appears open (search absence only). The closest precedents are simulated SO-101 entries that used an LLM or VLM over scripted primitives with a shared staging zone, one claiming 100% cup-handoff success over 10 sim seeds ([lablab entry](https://lablab.ai/ai-hackathons/ai-infra-summit-hackathon/inzuppato/bimanual-pick-handoff-place-with-vlm-planning), verified only via search snippet).

The single most important honesty point is physical: **a real arm cannot hand a part to a simulated arm**. Collaboration must therefore be shown on two real SO-101s, while scaling is shown with simulated arms. That reconciles the "one real arm minimises risk" instinct with the collaboration requirement: two real arms form one physical kitting cell, and simulated arms form additional virtual cells and parallel stations fed by the same orchestrator. Every bar and every plot point is labelled REAL or SIM.

A good task is **kit assembly**: each kit needs a base block placed in a fixture, a peg inserted while the base is held (hold-and-insert), and the finished kit moved to an output tray outside arm 1's reach (a forced handoff). The narrative then runs in six beats. First, a spoken or typed order ("six kits, blue pegs first") becomes a JSON DAG on screen, validated against a fixed skill library, with a cached plan as fallback if the API fails on stage, a failure mode earlier simulated-SO-101 teams hit with rate limits ([lablab profile](https://lablab.ai/u/@ari517)). Second, CP-SAT assigns the graph and the Gantt shows the plan, the lower bound and the optimality gap. Third, the two real arms execute their share while simulated arms execute theirs, with handoff bars aligned across lanes as visible sync points. Fourth, someone knocks a peg out of a gripper or unplugs an arm: the grasp check or heartbeat fires, the plan re-solves in about a second, and bars visibly move to other arms. Fifth, makespan before and after the failure is printed. Sixth, one slide plots makespan against N = 1, 2, 4, 8 from the simulated sweep with the real N = 1 and N = 2 points overlaid, the ideal 1/N line, and the gap annotated by cause: critical path, zone waits, sync waits, orchestrator overhead.

Three comparisons make the numbers credible. Report **one arm sequential, HEFT greedy and CP-SAT** on the same task, with trial count and real wall-clock seconds, since a credible claim states its baseline, arm count and trials. Show **predicted versus measured per-skill durations on the real arms**, which validates that the simulated arms' timing is not invented. And if time allows, show the nontrivial result that with a split workspace **a dedicated handoff "runner" arm can beat N independent pickers**, or show a HEFT list-scheduling anomaly where an extra arm made greedy worse while CP-SAT did not (both inference, not checked; run them before claiming them). Two to three times faster with four arms is in line with every published system; anything near N-times invites the question of what was left out.

## Recommended architecture and the 48-hour build plan

### Seven components, each with a fallback

| Layer | Recommended choice | Fallback if it slips |
|---|---|---|
| Task spec | JSON DAG: tasks, skill, object ids, allowed arms, zones, `needs_arms` (1 or 2), `relation` (handoff, during), durations per arm | Hand-written JSON for the demo kits |
| Language front end | One LLM call to DAG (OSDAG pattern), JSON-schema validated against skill library; LeRobot's `VlmPlanner` prompt style as template ([planner.py](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/blob/d3793b7e36522321c1c455f0dc4826db3793dceb/src/lerobot/rollout/planner.py)) | Cached DAG per demo order |
| Planner | Raw OR-Tools CP-SAT: optional interval per (task, arm), shared literal for arm pairs, NoOverlap per arm and per zone, precedence with lags, `during` for hold-and-insert, minimise makespan, 5 s limit, 8 workers, HEFT hint; lower bound reported | HEFT (also the baseline) |
| Executor | Event-driven partial-order dispatcher; runtime zone locks; barriers for two-arm steps; re-solve on failure with fixed history, 1-2 s limit | HEFT re-dispatch of remaining tasks |
| Arm workers | One process per arm, `execute(skill) -> result`; backends: real `SOFollower` (udev symlink, unique id) or MuJoCo copy at 1x wall clock; durations from measured p80 (real) or the same measured durations (sim) | Fewer sim arms if CPU or rendering lags |
| Skills | Scripted placo IK: pick, place, present, take, hold, insert, park; grasp check by gripper position plus `Present_Current`; timeout 1.5x predicted | Taught joint-space waypoints |
| Visualisation | Plotly/Dash plan-vs-actual Gantt plus scaling plot for judges; Rerun StateTimeline, DAG graph and 3D for engineers | Static Gantt PNG regenerated per run |

Everything else can change behind them.

## Conclusion

The intellectually honest pitch is not "AI splits work across robots", which RoCo, SMART-LLM, RoboPARA and OSDAG have all shown in simulation, but "**a decades-old scheduling formulation, given real measured durations, re-splits a collaborative assembly across cheap real arms when one fails, and tells you exactly why it stops scaling**". The field has the algorithms and lacks the measurements: published multi-arm systems report success rates or simulated makespan, while real wall-clock makespan with failures on low-cost hardware is close to empty territory. That reframes the hackathon from a robotics-capability contest, where SO-101s lose to Frankas, into a systems-and-evidence contest, where a cheap rig with honest numbers can win.

Two implications reach beyond the weekend. First, schedule quality on cheap arms is bounded by duration accuracy more than by solver cleverness, because CP-SAT is already within about 1% of best-known on benchmark job shops; a calibrated per-arm twin that predicts skill durations is therefore the lever that turns this demo into a product. Second, the scaling curve itself is the most transferable artefact: a lower bound that names the binding constraint (arms, task graph or shared zone) tells a factory whether to buy another arm, redesign the fixture, or re-cut the task, which is the decision that integrators currently make by hand.
